## What changed mid-flight

**FR4 was cut.** The Epic opened with four functional requirements and shipped three; the 48-entry
inbox triage moved to **M5.E14** to travel with the tracker migration (`D-M5E17-3`). The recorded
reason is that six consecutive M5 Epics had been Signal working on Signal, and the inbox wall needs
sustained product judgment rather than another pass by the same hands.

**Two of the Epic's five decisions were not about its own subject matter.** `D-M5E17-4` and
`D-M5E17-5` — the relative `.` marketplace source, and PR discipline as a constant with Epic
ceremony as the variable — emerged at the very end and changed how *every* subsequent change reaches
`main`. They carry this Epic's number but were provoked by `B58`, a fix-lane P1 found the same day.
Ironically, they landed hours **after** this Epic's own release went direct to `main`.

## What assumptions broke

**"The three verdict statements contradict each other."** They did not — `review.md`'s three
statements agreed perfectly *with each other*. The contradiction was between all three and shipped
practice (M5.E9 and M5.E13 had both shipped PASS-WITH-FIXES with an in-phase Critical). This is why
AC2.1 was satisfiable by a no-op, and it reframed the fix: the **rule was miscalibrated, not the
practice** (`D-M5E17-1`).

**"There are three sibling call sites."** FR3.2 named three; the sweep found **five**, landing at
2 explicit / 2 silent / 1 wrong. Stating the expected number before looking is what made the gap
legible — had the requirement said "the siblings" instead of "three," the two extra sites would have
been covered silently or missed silently, with no way to tell which.

**"A red baseline can be predicted."** It was measured instead — AC3.1′ 3 of 5 failing, AC2.1′ 3 of
3 — and measuring it is what caught the next item.

## What surprised us

**Measuring the red baseline caught an error in the measuring instrument.** A naive regex false-RED'd
`plan.md` and `discuss.md`, the two files that were already correct, because they read
`Run it **after** the commit` and the markdown bold broke the match. The commit records the lesson
in one line: *a test that fails on correct input gets "fixed" by weakening it.* The probe was
corrected to strip emphasis.

**And the one that only became visible three days later: an Epic about instructions contradicting
other instructions skipped three of its own phases and its own retrospective, and nothing said so
for two days.** `B71` reconstructed it from git: at the ship commit, `STATE.md` read `phase: PLAN`
with `completed_phases: [DISCUSS (2026-07-31)]`, and `MILESTONE-5.md` contained **zero** E17 rows.
Both Epic-close detectors return `false` on that shape — `isEpicCloseShip` needs a milestone row
that did not exist, and `isEpicCloseByState` needs `phase === 'SHIP'` plus a complete pre-SHIP
ledger and had **one of five**. The gate did not fail; it was never reachable.

## What we'd do differently

**Invoke `/sig:ship` instead of hand-cutting the release.** Every process defect above traces to one
root: the release was assembled by hand, so every gate living inside the command was bypassed by not
running it. Nothing was overridden — the code was simply never asked.

**Write `PROGRESS` during EXECUTE rather than trusting the commit message.** This retro is
reconstructible only because `137b9ca`'s author wrote an unusually complete message. That is luck,
not design, and the next compressed Epic will not necessarily be so generous.

**Separate the tier decision for content from the tier decision for process.** FEATURE with
`gate_strictness: light` was right for the *content* — three document corrections, low blast radius
— and wrong for the *process*, because "light" is where a two-commit Epic stops tripping anything.
The risk of a compressed Epic is not proportional to the size of its diff.

## What to feed back into Signal

**`B71` is the mechanism finding, and it is already homed** at M5.E18 open question 3. The general
shape: both Epic-close detectors verify one document's claim against another document's claim, never
against the world. *Did a release actually get cut?* is answerable from git — a version bump, a tag,
a CHANGELOG entry — and neither detector asks. That is the same class M5.E13 and M5.E16 each named,
in a third shape.

**Coverage is inversely correlated with risk here, which is the part worth generalizing.** An Epic
that runs all six phases is the one least likely to skip its retro, and it is the only shape the
gate can see. Any future gate should be checked against the case that *skips* the path it lives on.

**FR1's own contribution — name what the Epic does for the first time and schedule it in wave 1** —
is the durable output, and this Epic never got to test it. M5.E16 did, one release later.

**`B56` was filed during this Epic's PLAN**, incidentally, while establishing the test baseline —
`references/facts.md` publishes a test count nothing derives from the suite. Found by a human
reading a number, not by a mechanism, which is the recurring way these surface.

**Process note for backfills:** this file is the first retro in the project written after the fact
from external records. If that becomes a pattern rather than a one-off, the reconstruction preamble
should become a template section rather than hand-written prose each time — and if it becomes
common, that is itself the signal that `/sig:ship` is being bypassed routinely.

## Anti-rationalization moment

**Two acceptance criteria turned out to be satisfiable by doing nothing, and both were re-executed
rather than claimed.** AC2.1 asked that `review.md`'s three verdict statements agree — they already
did. AC3.1 asked that a command "does not instruct `markFresh` before the commit" — which a file
stating *no ordering at all* passes trivially, by silence. The easy path was available and
defensible: the criteria were met as written, the suite was green, and nobody would have checked.
`137b9ca` records that both were rewritten into forms that could actually fail, and re-run. The
Epic's subject was instructions that pass while meaning nothing; declaring victory on two criteria
that pass while meaning nothing would have made the Epic an instance of its own defect.

**The second one is smaller and sharper.** When the red-baseline probe failed on the two files that
were already correct, the fastest repair was to loosen the regex until the suite went green. That is
the move the commit message names and refuses in the same sentence — *a test that fails on correct
input gets "fixed" by weakening it* — and the probe was corrected to strip markdown emphasis
instead.

**What this retro did not get to record:** whether any similar moment occurred during VERIFY or
REVIEW, because neither phase left an artifact. That absence is the finding, not a gap in the
writing.
